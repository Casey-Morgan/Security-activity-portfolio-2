# B5 - Cybersecurity Related Talks and Seminars

## Overview

This entry documents two cybersecurity-related lectures. The first lecture focused on real-world financial crime, fraud and scam trends, and the way AI is being weaponised by attackers. The second lecture focused on AI in defensive cybersecurity, including machine learning, anomaly-based intrusion detection systems, and large language models.

---

## Lecture 1 - Financial Crime in the Real World

### Speaker

Wellington Muvunduke

### Main Topic

Financial crime in the real world, fraud and scam trends, and how AI is being weaponised.

### Summary

This lecture discussed how financial crime appears in real-world environments and how fraud and scam activity continues to change over time. The lecture highlighted that financial crime is not only a technical issue, but also a social, organisational, and behavioural problem.

A major focus of the lecture was the way attackers use deception to manipulate victims. Fraud and scams often rely on urgency, fear, trust, impersonation, and emotional pressure. These techniques can be used against individuals, businesses, and financial institutions.

The lecture also discussed how artificial intelligence is being weaponised. Attackers can use AI to create more convincing phishing messages, generate fake identities, automate scam communication, imitate writing styles, and make fraudulent content appear more realistic. This makes scam detection harder for normal users and increases the pressure on cybersecurity teams to improve awareness, monitoring, and response.

### Key Topics Covered

- real-world financial crime;
- fraud and scam trends;
- social engineering;
- phishing and impersonation;
- financial loss caused by cyber-enabled scams;
- AI-generated scam content;
- attackers using AI to scale deception;
- the need for stronger detection and awareness.

### What I Learned

I learned that many cyber incidents are connected to human behaviour as much as technology. Attackers do not always need advanced technical exploits if they can trick a person into clicking a link, sending money, sharing credentials, or trusting a fake identity.

I also learned that AI can increase the realism and scale of scams. For example, a scam message written by AI may have fewer spelling mistakes and may sound more professional than older scam messages. This means users cannot rely only on obvious warning signs such as poor grammar.

Another important lesson was that financial crime prevention requires multiple layers of defence. These include user education, transaction monitoring, identity checks, suspicious activity reporting, strong authentication, and quick response when fraud is suspected.

### Cybersecurity Relevance

This lecture is highly relevant to cybersecurity because financial crime often depends on cyber-enabled methods such as phishing, account takeover, identity theft, business email compromise, fake websites, and malicious links.

Understanding fraud and scam trends helps defenders design better awareness training and detection controls. It also shows why cybersecurity professionals need to understand attacker behaviour, not just technical vulnerabilities.

## Lecture 2 - AI in Defensive Cybersecurity

### Speaker

Larry Huynh

### Main Topic

AI in Defensive Cybersecurity, Machine Learning, Anomaly-based Intrusion Detection Systems, and Large Language Models.

### Summary

This lecture focused on how artificial intelligence can support defensive cybersecurity. The lecture discussed machine learning, anomaly-based intrusion detection systems, and large language models.

The lecture explained that modern security teams often need to analyse large amounts of data from endpoints, networks, cloud platforms, identity systems, and applications. AI and machine learning can assist by identifying suspicious patterns, detecting unusual behaviour, prioritising alerts, and helping analysts understand security events more efficiently.

Anomaly-based intrusion detection was an important part of the lecture. Instead of only looking for known attack signatures, anomaly-based systems try to identify behaviour that is different from normal activity. This can help detect new or unusual attacks, but it can also produce false positives if normal behaviour changes.

The lecture also discussed large language models and their possible use in defensive workflows. LLMs can help summarise alerts, explain technical findings, draft incident reports, support security education, and assist analysts during investigations. However, they need careful human review because they may make mistakes or provide incomplete answers.

### Key Topics Covered

- artificial intelligence in cybersecurity;
- machine learning for security detection;
- anomaly-based intrusion detection systems;
- identifying unusual behaviour;
- large language models in defensive workflows;
- alert summarisation and investigation support;
- false positives and false negatives;
- the need for human oversight.

### What I Learned

I learned that AI can help defenders manage the large volume of data produced by modern systems. Machine learning can be useful when patterns are difficult to detect manually, especially in environments with many users, devices, and network events.

I also learned that anomaly-based detection is powerful but not perfect. A system may flag harmless unusual behaviour as suspicious, or it may miss an attack that looks similar to normal behaviour. This means AI-based security systems need tuning, evaluation, and human judgement.

The discussion on LLMs showed that they can be useful assistants for cybersecurity work, but they should not be treated as fully reliable decision-makers. Security analysts still need to verify outputs, check evidence, and make responsible decisions.

### Cybersecurity Relevance

This lecture is relevant to defensive cybersecurity because AI is increasingly used in modern security operations. Machine learning and anomaly detection can help identify threats faster, while LLMs can support analysis, documentation, and learning.

The lecture also showed that AI has risks and limitations. Effective defensive use of AI requires careful design, testing, monitoring, and human review.

## Overall Reflection

These two lectures helped me understand both the offensive and defensive sides of modern cybersecurity.

The lecture by Wellington Muvunduke showed how financial crime works in the real world and how fraud and scam trends are changing. It highlighted that AI is not only a defensive tool; it can also be weaponised by attackers to create more convincing scams and increase the scale of deception.

The lecture by Larry Huynh showed how AI can also help defenders. Machine learning, anomaly-based intrusion detection systems, and large language models can support detection, investigation, and reporting. However, these tools still require human judgement because AI can make mistakes or produce uncertain results.

Together, the two lectures showed that AI is becoming important on both sides of cybersecurity. Attackers may use AI to improve scams, while defenders may use AI to detect threats and respond more effectively. This means cybersecurity professionals need to understand both the risks and the benefits of AI.
