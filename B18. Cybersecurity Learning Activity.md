# B21 - Cybersecurity Learning Activity

## Learning Activity Title

Phishing Message Spotting Challenge

## Overview

This activity is a beginner-friendly cybersecurity learning activity designed to teach people how to recognise phishing messages. The activity uses realistic but fictional examples of scam messages and asks learners to identify warning signs.

The activity is practical because phishing is one of the most common cyber threats faced by students, families, and organisations. It can lead to stolen passwords, financial loss, account takeover, malware infection, and identity theft.

---

## Target Audience

This activity is designed for beginner learners, such as:

- university students;
- friends;
- family members;
- high school students;
- general internet users.

No technical background is required.

---

## Learning Goals

By the end of the activity, learners should be able to:

1. explain what phishing is;
2. identify common warning signs in suspicious messages;
3. avoid clicking unknown links;
4. verify messages using official sources;
5. explain why passwords and verification codes should not be shared.

---

## Topic Explanation

Phishing is when an attacker sends a fake message pretending to be a trusted person or organisation. The attacker usually wants the victim to click a link, open an attachment, enter a password, share a verification code, or send money.

A simple safety rule taught in this activity is:

```text
Stop, check, and verify before clicking.
```

This means:

- **Stop:** Do not rush or panic.
- **Check:** Look carefully at the sender, link, wording, and request.
- **Verify:** Use the official app, official website, or trusted contact method.

## Warning Sign Checklist

Learners are taught to look for these warning signs:

- unexpected message;
- urgent or threatening wording;
- spelling or grammar mistakes;
- suspicious links;
- unknown sender;
- requests for passwords;
- requests for verification codes;
- payment requests;
- offers that sound too good to be true;
- attachments from unknown senders.

---

## Activity Instructions

### Step 1 - Explain the Topic

Start with this short explanation:

```text
Phishing is a fake message designed to trick you.
It may pretend to be from your bank, delivery company, university,
government service, or someone you know. The goal is usually to make you click a link,
give away a password, share a code, or send money.
```

### Step 2 - Teach the Safety Rule

Explain:

```text
Before clicking anything, stop, check, and verify.
```

Give an example:

```text
If a message says your bank account is locked, do not click the link.
Open the official banking app yourself or call the bank using the official phone number.
```

### Step 3 - Run the Message Spotting Challenge

Show learners the example messages below. Ask them to decide whether each message is safe, suspicious, or needs verification.

### Step 4 - Discuss the Answers

Ask learners which warning signs they noticed and what they would do next.

### Step 5 - Complete the Mini Quiz

Use the quiz at the end of the activity to check understanding.

---

## Example Message 1

```text
Your parcel could not be delivered. Pay $2.99 redelivery fee now:
http://auspost-redelivery-check.example
```

### Expected Answer

Suspicious.

### Warning Signs

- unexpected payment request;
- urgent wording;
- suspicious link;
- tries to imitate a delivery service;
- asks the user to act quickly.

### Safer Action

Do not click the link. Open the official Australia Post app or website directly.

---

## Example Message 2

```text
Your university account will be locked today. Confirm your login now:
http://student-login-secure.example
```

### Expected Answer

Suspicious.

### Warning Signs

- threatens account lockout;
- creates urgency;
- asks for login details;
- link may not be the real university website;
- could steal student credentials.

### Safer Action

Do not click the link. Visit the university website directly or contact the IT helpdesk.

---

## Example Message 3

```text
Hi, your bank detected unusual activity. Reply with the six-digit code we sent you
 so we can protect your account.
```

### Expected Answer

Suspicious.

### Warning Signs

- asks for a verification code;
- impersonates a bank;
- creates fear about account security;
- asks the user to reply directly.

### Safer Action

Never share verification codes by SMS. Open the official bank app or call the bank using the official phone number.

---

## Example Message 4

```text
Reminder: Your dentist appointment is tomorrow at 10:00 am.
 Please call the clinic directly if you need to change it.
```

### Expected Answer

Likely safer, but still verify if unexpected.

### Warning Signs

There are fewer warning signs because:

- there is no link;
- it does not request a password;
- it does not ask for payment;
- it suggests calling the clinic directly.

### Safer Action

If unsure, call the clinic using a phone number from its official website.

---

## Mini Quiz

### Question 1

What is phishing?

```text
Answer: A fake message designed to trick someone into clicking a link, opening a file, sending money, or sharing private information.
```

### Question 2

What should you do before clicking a link in an unexpected message?

```text
Answer: Stop, check the message, and verify through an official source.
```

### Question 3

Should you share a verification code with someone who says they are from your bank?

```text
Answer: No. Verification codes should not be shared.
```

### Question 4

Name two warning signs of a phishing message.

```text
Answer examples: urgent wording, suspicious link, unknown sender,
spelling mistakes, password request, payment request, verification code request.
```

### Question 5

What should you do if a message says your university account will be locked?

```text
Answer: Do not click the link. Go directly to the university website or contact IT support.
```

## Reflection

Designing and implementing this learning activity helped me practise explaining cybersecurity in a simple and practical way. Phishing can be technical, but the main defensive lesson can be taught using everyday examples such as delivery messages, bank alerts, and university account warnings.

The most effective part of the activity was the message spotting challenge because it required active participation. Instead of only reading advice, the learner had to decide whether each message was safe or suspicious.

I also learned that a good cybersecurity learning activity should give clear actions, not just warnings. The rule **stop, check, and verify** is easy to remember and can help people avoid scams in real life.

---

## Ethical and Defensive Scope

All phishing examples used in this activity are fictional. No real phishing website was created, no real malicious link was used, and no learner was asked to enter personal information.

The activity was designed only for cyber safety education and defensive awareness.
